# Period English for reviewers

Read this while reading a book for slips. Early-modern and later English
looks wrong to modern eyes in ways that are not errors; scanning makes
errors that look like real words. Tell them apart before changing anything,
and check a suspect against the early printing.

## Contents

1. Scanning and OCR confusions (errors)
2. Long s and old letter forms (errors in a modern text)
3. Grammar that is period, not wrong (keep)
4. Words whose meaning has changed (keep; gloss only if asked)
5. Spellings: period variants and modern forms
6. Latin, Greek and Hebrew

## 1. Scanning and OCR confusions

Found in copies made from scanned 19th-century editions (Sibbes) and in
transcriptions of worn print. Each pair: what the scan gives → what was
printed. They produce real words, so a spell checker passes them; only
sense and the early printing catch them.

| Confusion | Examples seen |
| --- | --- |
| b ↔ h | bath/hath, bead/head, bad/had, be bad/he had, hear/bear |
| e ↔ c, o | tho/the, yon/you, clisertos/disertos |
| he ↔ be | "if so he", "there shall he no more", "they he struck" |
| f ↔ ſ (long s) ↔ l | lied/fled, title/little, lore/love |
| rn ↔ m, in ↔ m | miner/miser, optinus/optimus |
| cl ↔ d, li ↔ h | clisertos/disertos |
| u ↔ n | Sapieus/Sapiens, Indus/ludus |
| d ↔ cl, o ↔ e | sold/soul, wall/will, owe/our |
| word splits and joins | "vouch safeth", "de-rideth", "form" for "for me", "be bad" |
| dropped letters | Gentles/Gentiles, Tertulhan/Tertullian, ill/will |
| quote/apostrophe slips | "sacrificed,' wisdom" (opening quote set as closing), `’’` for `”` |

When a word is grammatical but makes nonsense ("the church lied into the
wilderness", "we have united for him"), suspect a scan before an author.

## 2. Long s and old letter forms

In a modern-spelling edition these must not survive: `ſ` (long s; often
misread as f: "faid" for "said", "fome" for "some"), `ʋ`/`Ʋ` for v/U,
`vv` for w, `&c.` (write "etc."), `ye` with a superscript e meaning
"the" (thorn), `ꝑ`, macrons (`frō` = from, `thē` = them, `cōmand` =
command). The early printing's `&` is "and".

## 3. Grammar that is period, not wrong (keep)

- Verb forms: hath, doth, saith, -eth (knoweth, loveth), thou -est
  (teachest, couldest, shouldest, wouldest), art, wast, wert, shalt, wilt,
  canst, durst, wist, holpen, spake, brake, bare (bore), sware, gat,
  digged, eat (ate), wrought, strucken.
- Pronouns: thou, thee, thy, thine (before a vowel: "thine eyes"), ye (as
  subject), mine (before a vowel: "mine eye"), 'em, his (for its).
- Constructions: "that that" (that which), "a-praying", "a-sleeping",
  double negatives ("nor no threatenings"), "he don't", "these be", "they
  be", "it is" + plural, "an" before h ("an hope"), "an" or "and" meaning
  if, "as" for "that", "which" for "who", "more better", "the which",
  "for to", "him that" for "he that".
- Contractions: 'tis, 'twas, 'twill, ne'er, o'er, e'er.
- Inversions and verbless clauses in sermons ("Use 1.", "Obs.", "Quest.",
  "Ans.", "Reason 1.").

A light edition keeps all of this. Offer plain English only where a
sentence reads as a mistake to a modern reader ("other men, good wit" →
"other men of good wit"; "that we not fear" → "that we do not fear"), and
ask first.

## 4. Words whose meaning has changed (keep)

prevent (go before), let (hinder), conversation (conduct, way of life),
meat (food), quick (living), corn (grain), idiot (layman), ingenious /
ingenuous (frank), artificial (skilful), curious (careful), presently
(at once), anon (soon), by and by (immediately), silly (simple, innocent),
nice (fastidious), naughty (worthless), lewd (base), mortify (put to
death), communicate (share), discover (reveal), comfortable (strengthening),
admire (wonder at), wit (mind), fond (foolish), sad (serious), sometime
(once, formerly), still (always), indifferent (impartial), suffer (allow),
want (lack), wont (accustomed), withal (with it, besides), betimes
(early), ere (before), whither (where to), whence, wherefore, howbeit,
peradventure (perhaps), the rather (the more), "beholding" (beholden).

## 5. Spellings

A modern-spelling edition writes modern spellings of the same word
(labour/labor and Saviour/Savior are both modern: the book chooses one and
keeps to it) but keeps different words (shew is a spelling of show; "eat"
as a past tense is a different form — keep). Watch for:

- the same word spelled two ways in one book (every one / everyone, to-day
  / today, any thing / anything, Saviour / Savior, Mat / Matt);
- KJV forms of biblical names (Elijah or Elias as the book prints;
  Sodom, Zion or Sion: one form);
- a spelling that is a different word (practice noun / practise verb in
  British spelling; "their" / "there"; "then" / "than", which early
  printers used interchangeably — modernize to the sense).

## 6. Latin, Greek and Hebrew

Puritan texts quote Augustine, Bernard, Jerome, Tertullian and classical
tags. Check spelling against the standard form (`Laetitia habet suas
lachrymas`, `Calumniare audacter, aliquid haerebit`, `Bonus ludus`, `Deus
est optimus arbiter`, `Pectus facit disertos`, `Sapientis non est
admirari`), keep the author's word order, and check that the translation
beside it is faithful and plain ("a bad work in a good matter", not "bad
work with regard to a good matter/occasion"). Greek needs its accents and
breathings (αὐτόπιστοι, ἀξιόπιστοι, καὶ, τῷ); Hebrew transliterations
stay as printed. When a TCP transcription shows `〈 in non-Latin alphabet
〉`, the early printing gives no evidence: the copy's reading stands unless
a page image says otherwise.
