# Working with EEBO-TCP texts (quod.lib.umich.edu source)

This is the second book in the imprint set from EEBO-TCP. The first was
William Perkins' *Christian Oeconomie* (`A09377`, 30,000 words); this is
William Gouge's *Of Domesticall Duties* (`A68107`, 290,000 words). Everything
in `../../../william-perkins/christian-economy/sources/CLAUDE.md` still holds — read it
first. This file records what changed at ten times the size, and it is the
version to copy for a new book.

## The source

`https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/<ID>.0001.001/1:N?rgn=div1;view=fulltext`
is a web front-end over the **Text Creation Partnership** corpus. **The site
blocks bot/programmatic access** — don't fight it. Every EEBO-TCP text has a
matching GitHub repo with the plain TEI-XML:

```
git clone --depth 1 https://github.com/textcreationpartnership/<ID>
```

The `1:N` in the URL is the index into the flattened list of direct `<div>`
children of `<front>`, `<body>` and `<back>`. `tcp_to_typst.py --list` prints
that mapping; the numbers are what `--names` and `--sections` take.

Before assuming a text has only one witness, check the neighbouring TCP ids for
another edition of the same work — there is none for this one (A68105/6/8/9 are
unrelated books), which is why the six-page lacuna below could not be closed.

## One renderer, not two

Perkins had `tcp_to_typst.py` for the old-spelling render and
`modernize_render.py` for the modernized one: two near-identical tree walkers
kept in step by hand, which drifted (its own CLAUDE.md notes a fix that had to
be "reapplied by hand after a regeneration").

Here there is **one renderer with a `--modernize` flag**. Modernization is a
single call on the flat, linearized string, so the two renders cannot disagree
about structure. `sources/original-spelling/` is generated by the same command
without the flag, and is worth keeping: it is what you diff against when the
modernizer does something surprising.

Files:

- **`tcp_to_typst.py`** — the renderer. Generic across EEBO-TCP; the
  book-specific parts are four explicitly-named tables (`MACRON_M_OVERRIDE`,
  `TEXT_FIXES`, `HEAD_FIXES`, and `DIV_LEVELS`/`RUN_IN_DIVS`).
- **`edition.json` + `build_edition.py`** — how the work is cut into printed
  volumes and chapters, and the build that follows from it. See "Cutting a
  book into volumes" below.
- **`gap_resolver.py`** — reconstructs illegible spans. Mostly generic; its
  `GAP_FIXES` is per-book.
- **`spelling.py`** — the word-level Early Modern → Modern spelling engine.
  Generic rules plus per-book `MANUAL`/`NAMES`/`LATIN_SKIP`.
- **`modernize.py`** — the prose layer: sentence capitalization, noun
  lowercasing, and Latin-run detection.
- **`wordlist.py`** — the tool for tuning `spelling.py` to a new book.

## What 290,000 words changes

### 1. Illegible gaps stop being a hand job (`gap_resolver.py`)

Perkins had 39 illegible `<gap>`s and they went into a hand-written table. This
book has 479. But it is also a big book, and **almost every damaged word occurs
undamaged somewhere else in it**. So: build a frequency list of the book's own
vocabulary, then for each gap look for words matching
`<letters before><n unknown><letters after>` and take the answer when it is
unambiguous. That resolves about 60% mechanically, and resolves them into *this
document's own* spelling conventions rather than modern ones.

Three refinements earned their keep:

- **The bullet count is approximate.** TCP's own guidelines say so. Try the
  stated length first, then ±1, and — only for a single well-attested candidate
  — a good deal wider: `•ictions` is `afflictions`, four letters lost where two
  were marked.
- **A one-letter "illegible" is often not a letter.** It can be a blot or a
  broken word-space: `maketh a<gap/>glad father` is "a glad father", and
  `to other most <gap/>grieuous` is just "most grievous". Bridge those — but
  only when no word in the book fits the shape at all. Running the blot rule
  before the ambiguity check turns every `<gap/>ut of many` into "ut of many"
  instead of "but of many", because "ut" is a real word in the Latin margins.
- **One letter of context is enough to work with.** Requiring two skips a whole
  class: a lost letter of a two-letter function word (`a` + gap → "as",
  gap + `f` → "of"). It is also where the matcher is *least* reliable — with
  one letter it is deciding on raw word frequency alone, and frequency picks
  "for" over "faune on" and "of" over "on". About one in five of those is
  wrong, which is exactly why `--gap-report` exists.

**Read the report.** `--gap-report gaps.tsv` prints every gap with its context
and the candidates considered. Working down it is a couple of hours and it
moved this book from 60% to 99% filled (189 hand entries). Everything the
matcher got *wrong* is in `GAP_FIXES` too, with the reading noted — those are
the entries a future edit must not "clean up".

**Key `GAP_FIXES` by document position, not by resolution order.** This was
learned twice, painfully. Numbering gaps as they are resolved means that
rendering a subset of divs, or moving one gap type out of the pipeline,
silently renumbers every fix after it — and a wrong fix does not error, it just
splices the wrong letters into a different word. `_stamp_indices` writes the
document-order number onto every `<gap>` as an attribute, including the
foreign-script and duplicate ones the renderer drops, so the numbering does not
depend on renderer policy either. The attribute survives the `deepcopy` that
paragraph and list rendering does; an object identity would not.

### 2. u/v and i/j are a search problem, not a table (`spelling.py`)

Perkins listed `seruant`, `aduanced`, `saluation`, `obiection` and their
inflections by hand. At 8,500 distinct words that is endless and error-prone.

But u/v and i/j were not spelling differences in 1622 — they were one letter
each, chosen by position. So the modern form is recoverable by **search**: swap
them and accept the variant the dictionary recognises, breadth-first so the
smallest change wins, and only ever on a word the dictionary does not already
know. `_oracle_letterforms` replaced most of a manual table and generalises to
any EEBO-TCP text with no retuning. (`VVJsdome` needs `j`→`i` as well as
`VV`→`W`; all four substitutions are in play.)

What still needs a hand table, and always will: words differing by more than
letterform and a silent e (`perswade`, `pretious`), proper names (`NAMES`), and
words where the oracle finds a real but *wrong* modern word (`DO_NOT_TOUCH` —
roman numerals are made entirely of the letters it swaps).

### 3. Latin has to be detected, not listed (`modernize.py`)

Notes here are not all citations — Gouge's margins carry a running analytical
summary in English, and leaving those in 1622 spelling beside modernized text
reads as an accident. So notes are modernized, in case-preserving "plain" mode.

Which puts the spelling rules on top of a patristic library. The silent-e and
`-es`/`-s` rules are exactly wrong for Latin and they *fire*, because the
English word they produce is real: `lege` → "leg", `ille` → "ill", `disces` →
"discs", `posse` → "poss", `homines` → "hominess". Blocklisting the vocabulary
does not scale.

Decide **per run** instead. A run is the text between two pieces of inline
markup — a good proxy for a quotation, since Latin is nearly always italic. If
most of its words are not English *even after modernization*, leave the run
alone. Test the modernized form, not the raw one, or `3. Christs loue pure.`
reads as Latin. Never judge a run under four words: `Ibid.` and
`Aug. Epist. 121.` look like Latin by any measure and have nothing in them to
modernize. `LATIN_SKIP` then catches the short fragments sitting inside an
otherwise English note.

Note what this gives up: `vxor` stays `vxor` rather than becoming `uxor`.
Leaving the Latin as the compositor set it is the conservative choice and the
defensible one.

### 4. Structure the small book did not have

- **`<table>`/`<row>`/`<cell>`, none of which is tabular data.** They are the
  brace diagrams a 1622 compositor drew to show one thing dividing into
  several -- "Behold here the mutual relation betwixt {Christ, / The Church.}"
  -- plus a few genuine row-wise lists in the front matter. Set as Typst
  tables they read as neither: the label floats alone in a column and the
  branches sit in another with nothing to align to. **Set them as a lead-in
  and a list.** Five things that had to be got right:

  **Braces are read COLUMN by column, row-wise tables ROW by row**, and what
  tells them apart is whether any cell spans rows (71 of the 76 do). In a
  brace a column holding one cell is the label and becomes a paragraph; a
  column holding several is the branches and becomes an enumerated list.
  Labels stand on either side or both -- "The effect is noted partly as a
  {Confirmation of the truth / Declaration of the measure} of Christs love" --
  and column order keeps them in reading order.

  **Empty unspanned cells are spacers, and must be dropped before placement,
  not after.** Treating them as real columns pushes everything after them out
  of line: Treatise I's "was {Creator/Creature}, but here {Head/Body}" puts a
  blank where "Body" belongs and strands "Body" two columns further on, so it
  comes out after the sentence that follows the brace.

  **A branch is capitalized, a label is not.** A branch is set on its own line
  and opens like one; a label continues the sentence running through the
  diagram ("of an husband.", "but here", "all which he") and keeps the case
  the compositor gave it. Find the first letter *past any markup* -- the "e"
  of "#emph[" is not the word's first letter.

  **Cells carry their own numbering and punctuation.** Strip a leading "1. "
  (the enum marker supplies it, or you get "1. 1. The dignity") and a trailing
  comma; add a full stop only when nothing follows the list, or "Nourisheth"
  and "Cherisheth" get stopped before the "it." they run into.

  **A table that interrupts a sentence is flattened inline instead.** One of
  the 76 does: "a family consisteth of these three orders, {Husbands/Wives,
  Parents/Children, Masters/Servants} all which he reckoneth up". Breaking the
  paragraph there cuts the sentence in three and the resumption is capitalized
  as though it began one. `_text_follows()` is the test, and it is text
  *after* the table that gives it away.

- **Long s.** 45,352 of them. Fold `ſ` → `s` at the point text is pulled out of
  the XML, so nothing downstream — spelling rules, gap resolver, word list —
  ever has to know about it. Same for `VV` → `W`.
- **Heading levels.** 594 `section` divs and 242 `question` divs. Mapping a
  section to a chapter heading in the `fgbooks` template would open 594 recto
  pages. Sections are level 3 (a run-in subheading) and a `question` head is set
  as a bold run-in paragraph, not a heading at all — they are two words long
  ("Quest.", "Answ."). `DIV_LEVELS` and `RUN_IN_DIVS` are where that lives.
- **Top-level heads are a title and a subtitle run together**: "The first
  Treatise. AN EXPOSITION OF THAT PART OF SCRIPTURE out of which Domestical
  Duties are raised." Set whole at 22pt that is a wall of type, so split at the
  first sentence break and set the remainder centred underneath.
- **Foreign-script gaps.** 240 Greek and Hebrew words TCP could not key, nearly
  all a single word in a marginal citation. Dropped, along with the punctuation
  left dangling in front of them. There is no page image here to read them from
  and TCP's placeholder is noise in a reading edition. Perkins resolved its
  three from a second witness; that was affordable at three.

### 5. Two traps worth naming

- **`etree.tostring(child)` includes the element's tail**, so re-parsing it
  raises "Extra content at the end of the document". Use `copy.deepcopy` and
  clear the tail. And when you `remove()` a nested `<list>` from a copied
  `<item>`, lxml takes its tail with it — that tail is the item's own text
  resuming after the sub-list, so re-attach it to the previous sibling first.
- **Typst is still in code mode until a `#set` statement ends.** `#set par(...)
  #emph[x]` on one line is a syntax error ("the character `#` is not valid in
  code"); the line break is load-bearing. Compile at least one generated file
  before assuming a rendering helper works.

## Cutting a book into volumes (`edition.json`, `build_edition.py`)

795 pages will not perfect-bind as one block, so this work prints as four
volumes while staying one ebook. The arrangement lives in `edition.json` and
nothing else knows about it: `build_edition.py` writes the chapter files, the
four volume `.typ`s, the single ebook `.typ`, and the covers. **Edit the JSON,
never the generated files.**

**Cut chapters by ordinal position, not by the printed section number.**
Gouge's numbering is not trustworthy — Treatise IV prints "s. 15", "s. 43",
"s. 46" and "s. 58" twice each, Treatise VIII prints "s. 4" twice, and two
sections (the 19th of Treatise VII, the 27th of Treatise VIII) have no heading
at all. Three chapter boundaries were quietly one section wrong on the first
build for exactly this reason; the way to check is to print the first and last
section heading of every generated chapter and read them, which takes a minute
and catches it immediately.

**Covers are a second pass.** A spine needs a page count and a page count needs
a compiled interior, so `--covers` reads the count out of the PDF rather than
taking a number on trust. A stale `pages:` is a wrong spine and a wrong spine
is a wrecked print run.

**The `<short>` metadata is not just for the running head.** The fgbooks
template uses it for the table of contents as well, so truncating a title to
fit the header gives a contents page full of "A Master's Care for His
Servants'…". Full titles here are at most 54 characters and fit both.

**Chapter titles are an editorial addition, and should look like one.** Gouge
wrote treatises and numbered sections; a 133-section treatise needs somewhere
to stop, so `edition.json` groups sections under chapter titles. Every section
heading stays underneath at its own level, in his words — the chapter title is
a signpost over his sections, not a replacement for them. Keep it that way for
the next book: a reader must be able to see what the author wrote and what the
edition added.

## Tuning `spelling.py` for a new book

`wordlist.py` is the tool. It reads the *original-spelling* render, throws away
Typst markup and footnote bodies, and reports:

```sh
python3 wordlist.py original-spelling/*.typ --unchanged --min 3   # old spellings still unhandled
python3 wordlist.py original-spelling/*.typ --changed --min 2     # what the rules currently do
python3 wordlist.py original-spelling/*.typ --notes --changed     # the same, inside marginal notes
python3 wordlist.py x --check seruant                             # one word, and why
```

Work `--unchanged` down until what is left is proper names, possessives without
apostrophes, and `-eth`/`-est` verb forms whose root is already modern — all
three are expected and none is a bug. Then read `--changed` top to bottom: a
bad rule shows up there as a column of nonsense rather than hiding in 290,000
words. That pass is what caught `beleeue` → "beleeve", `ioyned` → "joyned",
`weakenesse` → "weakeness", and `Mary` → "Marry".

That last one is worth generalising: **a word can be a proper name capitalized
and an ordinary word otherwise.** This book spells the verb "marry" as
"marie"/"mary" and also talks about the Virgin Mary constantly, so spelling
alone is not enough — `AMBIGUOUS_NAMES` resolves it on capitalization and
sentence position.

The dictionary itself needs seeding: `pyspellchecker`'s frequency list is
missing British `-our`/`-ise` forms and a surprising number of ordinary plurals
(`mischiefs`). Without them the oracle either leaves an old spelling alone
because it cannot see the modern form exists, or keeps searching and finds
something else.

## Known gaps in this edition

- **Pages 191–196 of the 1622 edition are missing from the TCP text** — not
  illegible, absent: they were not captured on the microfilm. They fall in
  Treatise II part 1, on the making of a marriage contract (Volume II,
  "Seeking Marriage"). The renderer
  emits an editorial note in their place (`MISSING_NOTE`). Closing it needs a
  second copy, and there is no second TCP witness; the 1626 and 1634 editions
  on archive.org are the place to look, by the "second witness" method in the
  Perkins CLAUDE.md.
- **One illegible span remains unresolved**, rendered as `…`: a lost word in
  Treatise V §14 (Volume III, "Children Getting Parents' Permission"), in the
  answer beginning "For the loving of father and mother more than Christ". Everything else either resolved or was read off its own
  context — including the marginal "Ex opere operato, B—", which the citation
  two sentences before it identifies as Bellarmine.
- **The 1622 errata are printed but not applied.** They key to original page
  and line numbers, which cannot be resolved against this setting.

## Quick start for a new EEBO-TCP book

```sh
git clone --depth 1 https://github.com/textcreationpartnership/<ID>
python3 tcp_to_typst.py <ID>/<ID>.xml --list

# old spelling first: it is what wordlist.py reads and what you diff against
python3 tcp_to_typst.py <ID>/<ID>.xml --outdir orig --gap-report gaps.tsv
python3 wordlist.py orig/*.typ --unchanged --min 3

# then, once spelling.py is tuned and gaps.tsv has been worked down
python3 tcp_to_typst.py <ID>/<ID>.xml --outdir out --modernize

# and, if the book needs cutting into volumes, write an edition.json and
python3 build_edition.py
```

Rebuild per book: `MACRON_M_OVERRIDE` (inspect every occurrence — the glyph
does not say whether it stands for n or m), `GAP_FIXES`, `TEXT_FIXES`,
`HEAD_FIXES`, and `spelling.py`'s `MANUAL`/`NAMES`/`LATIN_SKIP`. The mechanical
rules, the letterform oracle, the gap matcher and the Latin detector should
carry over untouched.
